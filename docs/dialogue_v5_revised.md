## Prologue

Davos, Switzerland. January 25th, 2007. A few minutes past midnight. The conference rooms are empty. Hans Rosling gave a presentation this morning that made several economists reconsider assumptions they had held for decades.

You have a research question that none of the panels at this conference quite answered: why did South Korea and Nigeria, two nations at nearly identical starting points in 1952, diverge so dramatically by today? Your notebook contains a lot of other people's theories. None of them feel like the whole truth.

The hotel bar is almost empty. Two men sit with drinks and a laptop open between them, talking quietly. You recognise both from the morning session.

Ji-ho Shin is Korean, fifty-eight, Senior Fellow at the Korea Development Institute in Seoul. He grew up poor in Busan and watched his country become something his childhood self could not have imagined. Emeka Adeyemi is Nigerian, fifty-five, Professor of Development Economics at the University of Lagos. He has spent thirty years studying why Nigeria's oil wealth did not become Nigerian prosperity.

**Emeka:** You were in the morning session. Sit down. We were just getting to the part of the conversation that took us twenty years to be able to have properly.

**Ji-ho:** We have a version of this conversation every time we are at the same conference. Tonight we thought we would try explaining it to someone who does not already have a conclusion. The question you are working on, Korea and Nigeria, the divergence, we have been living inside that question from opposite sides of it for most of our professional lives. Let us start in 1952.

## Chapter One — The Divergence

**Emeka:** In 1952, South Korea and Nigeria had almost identical GDP per capita. Korea sat at $1,030 per person, Nigeria at $1,077. The difference was forty-seven dollars. If you had asked a development economist in 1952 to predict which would be richer in fifty years, many would have picked Nigeria. We had more land, more people, a larger agricultural base, and natural resources already being surveyed by the British. On paper, Nigeria had the stronger starting position.

**Ji-ho:** And Korea had less than nothing. The war that ended in 1953 killed approximately two and a half million Koreans and destroyed roughly half of the country's industrial infrastructure. When economists looked at the two countries in 1952, the honest assessment was that Korea had a worse starting position than Nigeria, not just a similar one. The divergence that followed is not the story of an obvious winner pulling ahead. It is the story of a country that should have lost finding a way to win, and a country that should have won finding a way to lose.

**Emeka:** Before we show you the data — I want to ask you something first. In 1952, if you had to bet your career on which of these two countries would be richer fifty years later, which would you have picked?

[Prediction prompt: South Korea / Nigeria]

[Chart appears: GDP per capita line chart, 1952–2007, Korea in gold, Nigeria in green. Both lines begin close together, Korea climbs steadily from the mid-1960s, Nigeria oscillates and ends at $2,014 while Korea reaches $23,348.]

**Ji-ho:** This shows fifty-five years of GDP per capita for both countries, one of the starkest economic divergences in modern history. The gap at the right edge, that twenty-one thousand dollar difference per person, did not exist in 1952. It was created year by year, by decisions each country made, by structures each country built or failed to build, and by external forces that acted on each economy differently.

**Emeka:** The question is when the lines began to separate, what was happening at that moment, and how each country handled the shocks that came afterward. Both lines are close together through the 1950s. It is in the 1960s that Korea's line begins to pull away.

*What do you want to examine first?*

[A] Show me when and why the lines started to separate.
[B] Show me how stable each country's growth was through the shocks.
[C] Move on, I want to understand the deeper economic reasons.

### Option A — The Moment of Divergence

[Chart updates: shaded band over 1965–1980 labelled "Critical Window." Vertical markers at 1962 "Korea: First Five-Year Plan" and 1967 "Nigeria: Oil exports begin." Additional shaded band 1967–1970 labelled "Biafran Civil War."]

**Ji-ho:** That shaded region, 1965 to 1980, is where the story begins. Both countries were making structural decisions in the same fifteen-year window that would determine where their lines ended up. Something worth noting before that window opened: between 1949 and 1950, under American occupation, Korea undertook a substantial redistribution of agricultural land. By the time industrialisation began, Korea had a relatively egalitarian agrarian base — rural households with secure land tenure and, for the first time, a direct stake in what their country built next. Many development economists consider this land reform a foundational precondition for what the Five-Year Plans were able to accomplish. In 1962, the Korean government launched its First Five-Year Plan, a government-directed industrial programme targeting textiles, steel, and chemicals. The state chose what Korea would produce and created the conditions for those industries to grow. Every five years there was a new plan. In 1967, Nigeria began exporting crude oil for the first time.

[editor note: Land reform placed in Option A rather than Ji-ho's opening speech. His opening describes Korea's position at the time of the war; the land reform predates that speech's focus and sits more naturally as context for the industrialisation discussion here.]

**Emeka:** And in that same year, 1967, Nigeria's civil war began. The Biafran conflict ran until 1970, killing approximately two million people and devastating the eastern region that contained much of Nigeria's most productive agricultural and oil-producing land. The critical window when Korea was launching its industrial transformation was the exact same window when Nigeria was trying to hold itself together as a country. Korea was building during peace. Nigeria was building during civil war.

**Ji-ho:** Two nations, same window, completely different conditions and completely different choices about what engine to use for growth. Korea chose to build manufacturing because it had no alternative. Nigeria did not choose oil the way Korea chose manufacturing — the oil was discovered, and it was already there. What Nigeria faced was a different kind of decision: what to do with the revenue a commodities windfall was suddenly generating. Every economic model in the 1960s said that resource revenue, invested strategically, would drive development. That logic was not unreasonable at the time. The lines began to separate almost immediately, and they never came back together.

### Option B — The Volatility Gap

[Chart updates: second panel below GDP chart showing growth rate bars over 5-year periods. Shaded band 1980–1987, dashed line at 1997. Korea's bars are consistently positive across decades, Nigeria's swing sharply between large positives and negatives.]

**Ji-ho:** The lower panel shows growth rates between the Gapminder data points. Korea's bars are consistently positive across every decade. That predictability was the result of a diversified, manufacturing-based economy that was not dependent on any single commodity. Banks could lend long-term, firms could plan multi-year training programmes, families could commit to their children's education with reasonable confidence about the future.

**Emeka:** Nigeria's bars swing dramatically. The pattern mirrors the global oil price. Nigeria's economic growth was not driven by what Nigeria produced or built, it was driven by a number set in London and Houston. A country that cannot predict its own GDP growth rate cannot build the long-term institutions development requires. Every time Nigeria began to construct something, an oil price movement would wipe out the budget that was funding it. The volatility was not just a symptom of underdevelopment, it was one of its primary causes.

**Ji-ho:** And I want to be honest about the 1997 dip on Korea's line. The Asian Financial Crisis hit Korea hard. GDP fell six percent in a single year. Unemployment rose to seven percent, which had massive social consequences in a country where lifetime employment had been almost universal. Suicide rates increased measurably. But the recovery happened in two years because Korea's economy had depth. Nigeria's 1982 crash, by contrast, was followed by more than a decade of stagnation. The difference is not that Korea avoided pain, it is what existed underneath each economy to absorb the pain when it arrived.

### Option C — The Oil Argument

**Emeka:** You are going to ask about the oil, because that is always the question. Nigeria had oil, oil means revenue, revenue means investment, investment means development. Why did that chain not hold? The oil did something to Nigeria that took us decades to understand properly.

**Ji-ho:** The economic literature calls it the Dutch Disease, named after the Netherlands in the 1960s, when the discovery of large North Sea natural gas reserves caused the Dutch manufacturing sector to collapse despite the country becoming wealthier in aggregate.

**Emeka:** Wealthier in the aggregate. Poorer in the parts that would have had to build what came next.

[editor note: Brief Emeka reaction inserted at the natural break between Ji-ho's Netherlands example and the mechanism explanation. The line is in his analytic register — dry, precise — and functions as an invitation for Ji-ho to continue rather than an interruption.]

**Ji-ho:** When a country discovers a highly profitable natural resource and begins exporting at scale, foreign currency floods in. The national currency strengthens. A strong currency makes every other export more expensive for the world to buy. Your textile factories become uncompetitive against cheaper imports, your manufacturing sector contracts, and gradually the only viable export left is the commodity itself.

**Emeka:** Nigeria's case was more severe than the Netherlands, and the consequences lasted far longer, because the oil did not just crowd out other sectors economically. It hollowed out the institutional architecture a modern economy requires. When a government can fund itself entirely from oil royalties, it does not need to build an effective tax system. When you do not need tax revenue, you do not need the institutions that collect it, a functioning civil service, an independent judiciary, regulatory agencies that protect markets and enforce contracts. Oil funded the government directly, bypassing every intermediary institution that, in most countries, is built because it is economically necessary. Korea had no oil and had to build all of those institutions because there was no other way to generate revenue to run the state. That pressure, uncomfortable as it was, forced us to construct the infrastructure of a modern economy.

## Chapter Two — The Structural Trap

**Ji-ho:** Before we look at the next chart, I want to be honest about how Korea actually executed the industrial policy I just described. The Five-Year Plans were implemented under Park Chung-hee, a general who took power in a military coup in 1961 and ruled Korea as a dictator until his assassination in 1979. Labour unions were suppressed. Opposition politicians were imprisoned. Journalists were censored. Workers' wages were deliberately kept low to maintain export competitiveness. The path Korea walked was effective. It was also, for long stretches, profoundly authoritarian. Any honest analysis has to include both facts.

**Emeka:** I appreciate you saying that, because the export complexity story usually gets told as if it were a pure economic accomplishment, when in fact it was a political bargain Korea made. Now, before we show you — your instinct. By 2007, what do you think Nigeria's exports looked like? Spread across different sectors, or concentrated in one?

[Prediction prompt: Diversified across different sectors / Concentrated in one]

[editor note: Prediction question integrated into the end of Emeka's existing speech rather than added as a new Ji-ho turn, replacing "Now let me show you the structural difference between what each country built." The invite stays in Emeka's voice and the flow is unbroken.]

[Chart appears: two stacked bar charts side by side. Korea's chart shows agriculture shrinking from 75% in 1962 to 2% in 2007, manufactures growing from 15% to 91%. Nigeria's chart shows oil growing from 8% in 1962 to 96% by 1982 and remaining there.]

**Emeka:** Korea's chart looks like a staircase. Every decade a new category of product grows to take more of the stack. Textiles replace raw agriculture in the 1960s, steel and chemicals appear in the 1970s, ships and heavy machinery in the 1980s, consumer electronics and semiconductors in the 1990s and 2000s. Each transition represents Korean workers acquiring new skills and Korean institutions adapting to support more complex industries.

**Ji-ho:** Nigeria's chart looks like a wall. One colour, essentially unchanged, from 1982 to 2007. Ninety-six percent of Nigerian exports were oil by 1982. Ninety-six percent in 2007. The country's entire economic structure froze the moment oil became profitable enough to make everything else uncompetitive.

*What do you want to examine?*

[A] Show me how these structures changed decade by decade.
[B] Show me the external conditions that made this divergence possible.
[C] I understand the structural difference. What does it mean for the people?

### Option A — The Staircase vs The Wall

[Chart updates: small multiples showing each decade as separate stacked bars.]

**Ji-ho:** Looking at each decade in sequence makes the pattern clearer. In 1962 Korea's exports are mostly agricultural. By 1972 manufactures have appeared. By 1982 they dominate. By 2007 Korea is one of the world's largest exporters of memory chips, televisions, and steel. The sequence was not accidental. The Korean government sat down every five years and asked what we could learn to make next. Then they built the universities, research institutes, and subsidised firms to get there. Authoritarianism made the prioritisation easier to enforce, which is part of why the political bargain I just mentioned was so consequential.

**Emeka:** Nigeria's sequence is different. In 1962 agricultural exports dominate. By 1967 oil appears. By 1982 it has swallowed everything else. Then the panels stop changing. 1992, 2002, 2007, the same amber-orange wall every time. Not because Nigerians stopped trying to build alternative sectors, but because the Dutch Disease mechanism was relentless. Every time a non-oil sector began to grow, oil's profitability made it uncompetitive. The wall is not a choice, it is what happens to an economy that finds oil before it has built anything else of substance.

### Option B — The Geopolitical Floor

[Chart updates: comparison chart showing US aid bars ($6.0B Korea vs $0.4B Nigeria) and parallel timeline with Korean War end, Five-Year Plan, OPEC boom, Biafran War, Oil crash, IMF Structural Adjustment 1986, and Asian Financial Crisis events.]

**Emeka:** Korea did not develop alone. Between 1953 and 1980, the United States provided Korea with approximately six billion dollars in grants and concessional loans, which adjusted for population was one of the largest per-capita foreign aid programmes in modern history. Korea also received preferential access to the American consumer market under Cold War trade arrangements, technology transfer from American and Japanese firms, and continuous military protection that removed the cost of defending against a hostile neighbour from the state budget.

**Ji-ho:** Each of those supports was real. I grew up in a country shaped by all of them.

[editor note: Brief Ji-ho insertion splitting Emeka's US aid speech before "None of this diminishes..." — breaks the long factual list from its framing, and the line is in Ji-ho's honest self-aware register without adding content he would not actually say.]

**Emeka:** None of this diminishes what Korean workers and institutions accomplished. It just describes the conditions under which they accomplished it.

**Ji-ho:** I have to acknowledge this, because every time I tell the story of Korean industrial policy without mentioning American aid I am telling a partial story. The Five-Year Plans were not fundable without American grant assistance in the 1950s and early 1960s. The export-led industrialisation model was not viable without preferential access to American consumer markets. These external supports created a floor underneath Korean development that allowed our domestic policy choices to actually work. Without that floor, the same choices would have produced very different outcomes.

**Emeka:** Nigeria inherited a different external context entirely. At independence in 1960, the oil contracts negotiated with multinational firms had concession terms that today would be considered profoundly asymmetric, but Nigeria had essentially no domestic legal expertise in petroleum contract law. The colonial-era export structures were inherited as functional realities. And when the 1982 oil price crash hit, Nigeria turned to the IMF for emergency lending, which came with the Structural Adjustment Programme imposed in 1986. The SAP mandated specific cuts to public spending on health, education, and food subsidies, exactly when investment in those areas was most needed for long-term development. Korea never faced structural adjustment with that level of conditionality.

**Ji-ho:** And then there is the revenue figure.

[editor note: Brief Ji-ho insertion before the corruption paragraph. Factual, neutral — opens the door without editorialising. The alternative was a brief Emeka sentence here, but a Ji-ho line avoids stacking two Emeka speeches with only a single-sentence Emeka bridge between them.]

**Emeka:** And I should add the corruption number directly. By credible estimates, approximately four hundred billion dollars of Nigerian oil revenue was lost to corruption between 1960 and 1999. That is real money that did not go to Nigerian schools, hospitals, or infrastructure. I am not going to pretend it was a small number or that it was someone else's fault. But oil revenue makes corruption possible at that scale because it bypasses the citizen-state accountability mechanism that tax-funded states have to maintain. If your revenue comes from drilling rather than from your population's earnings, your population has less leverage over how the revenue is spent. The oil did not cause the corruption, but it created the structural conditions in which corruption at that scale could occur without political consequences that would have stopped it.

### Option C — Bridge to Chapter Three

[Chart updates: combined comparison showing both countries' 2007 export baskets — Korea 91% manufactures, Nigeria 96% oil.]

**Emeka:** The export structure is a description of what each country's workforce spent their time doing, and therefore what skills they were developing, and what kind of economy they were building for the next generation. Korean workers in 2007 were designing semiconductors, engineering vehicles, writing software. These activities require continuous learning and continuous institutional investment.

**Ji-ho:** Nigerian workers in 2007, in the formal economy, were mostly employed in government, services, or oil-adjacent roles. The manufacturing sector that might have employed millions and trained them in complex skills had never been built at scale. Which brings us to the most important part of the story. Not what each country produced, but who was doing the work, who was being included in the economy, and what investments were being made in people rather than infrastructure or extraction.

## Chapter Three — The Demographic Dividend vs Burden

**Ji-ho:** There is a concept called the demographic dividend. When fertility falls, when families have fewer children, the ratio of working-age adults to dependants improves. Households have more savings per person. Governments have more revenue per person. More resources are invested in each individual child. Those better-educated children enter the workforce more productive than the previous generation, which accelerates growth, which leads to higher incomes, which leads to lower fertility. The cycle is self-reinforcing. Korea experienced this dividend in full. Nigeria did not.

**Emeka:** Before the chart traces these paths — which country do you think moved further along this trajectory? Fertility falling and life expectancy rising, both at once, over those fifty-five years.

[Prediction prompt: South Korea / Nigeria]

[Chart appears: connected scatter plot. X axis fertility rate reversed (7 on left, 1 on right). Y axis life expectancy 30–85. Korea's path begins at high fertility ~5.4 and life expectancy ~47 in 1952, sweeps right and upward over five decades to fertility 1.3 and life expectancy 78.6 in 2007. Nigeria's path moves mostly upward with little rightward movement, ending at fertility 6.0 and life expectancy 46.9.]

**Emeka:** Korea's path is long and sweeping, moving steadily toward lower fertility and higher life expectancy over five decades. By 2007 Korea has essentially completed the demographic transition. Nigeria's path moves upward, life expectancy improved from 36 to 47 years which is real human progress, but the path barely moves to the right at all. A Nigerian woman in 2007 still has, on average, six children. Almost identical to 1960.

**Ji-ho:** The fertility decline that triggers the demographic dividend never arrived. Because fertility stayed high, each dollar of oil revenue had to be divided among an ever-larger population. Each expansion of the healthcare system was immediately absorbed by a growing number of people who needed it. The dividend was never collected.

*What aspect do you want to examine?*

[A] Show me how Korea put all of its people, women as well as men, into the economy.
[B] Show me the difference in education investment between the two countries.
[C] Show me the bigger picture, was this just Korea and Nigeria or a wider pattern?

### Option A — The Gender Engine

[Chart updates: left panel shows female labour force participation, Korea rising from 35% to 51%, Nigeria flat at ~33-36%. Right panel shows dumbbell plot of male vs female secondary enrollment 1990 across several countries, Korea at 90/93% with a 3pp gap, Nigeria at 17/31% with a 14pp gap.]

**Ji-ho:** When Korea built those factories in the 1970s, and offices in the 1980s, and technology firms in the 1990s, women were on the floors, in the offices, in the engineering departments. The government invested in girls' education at the same rate as boys' from early in the industrialisation process. By 1990 the gender gap in Korean secondary enrollment was three percentage points. Statistically negligible. This matters economically in a specific way. If you have a hundred working-age people in your economy but structural barriers prevent fifty of them from participating fully, your economy is operating at half capacity. Korea found a way to use all hundred.

**Emeka:** Nigeria's gender gap in secondary education in 1990 was fourteen percentage points. Boys at 31%, girls at 17%. We were not simply losing the economic contribution of those girls. We were losing the savings they would have generated, the fertility decline their economic participation would have produced, and the next generation of educated workers they would have raised. It is a straightforward economic calculation. Nigeria was leaving a substantial portion of its human capital unused at the precise moment when oil revenue was making the urgency of that investment feel optional.

### Option B — The Education Outlier

[Chart updates: horizontal dot plot of secondary enrollment 1990 across approximately 30 developing countries. Korea highlighted in gold at 91%, near the Scandinavia reference line. Nigeria highlighted in green at 24%, in the lower-middle cluster.]

**Ji-ho:** By 1990 Korea's secondary enrollment was 91 percent, at the far right of the distribution, practically adjacent to Scandinavia. This is a country that in 1952 had a war-ruined education system and within forty years achieved enrollment rates comparable to Western Europe. This transformation required decades of sustained public investment, mandatory schooling laws, and a cultural emphasis on academic achievement that the government actively cultivated. When the economy needed engineers, Korea had engineers. When it needed semiconductor technicians, it had semiconductor technicians.

**Emeka:** Nigeria's enrollment in 1990 was 24 percent, in that cluster on the left, alongside countries that had a fraction of Nigeria's oil revenue. We had the money. Nigeria in the 1970s and 1980s was collecting enormous oil revenues. The economists advising the government knew what the enrollment rates were and what they needed to be. What did not exist was the institutional commitment to redirect that money toward long-term human capital investment. When a government's revenue comes from drilling rather than from taxing its citizens, it is less accountable to those citizens for how the revenue is spent. The oil removed the accountability mechanism at the same time as it removed the urgency.

### Option C — The Continental Picture

[Chart updates: two Plotly choropleth maps side by side. Left map GDP per capita 2007. Right map life expectancy 2007. East Asian countries darker on both, Sub-Saharan Africa pale on both.]

**Emeka:** Korea and Nigeria are one story, but they are also representative of something larger. In both maps the same geographic pattern appears. East Asia is significantly darker. Sub-Saharan Africa is almost uniformly pale. This is not about two countries that happened to diverge, it is about two regions that followed fundamentally different developmental paths over the same fifty-five years, and the reasons are broadly the same reasons we have been discussing.

**Ji-ho:** East Asia, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong, and increasingly China, pursued variations of the same strategy: export-led industrialisation, sustained education investment, demographic transition, female workforce participation. Sub-Saharan Africa, with important exceptions, remained in commodity dependency, whether oil in Nigeria, copper in Zambia, diamonds in the DRC, cocoa in West Africa. The colonial export structures were not replaced.

**Emeka:** I want to be careful here not to make this sound like a simple morality tale. Conditions were not equal. Colonial history left very different institutional inheritances. The Cold War geopolitics that gave Korea access to American markets was not available to most of sub-Saharan Africa in the same way. But the maps also show that some countries made more of what was possible than others. Understanding the difference, at the level of economic structure, education, and human capital, is what this conversation has been trying to show you.

## Final Dissertation

[The two characters speak in alternating turns, each responding to what the other has just said. Ji-ho begins.]

[editor note: Staging updated from simultaneous parallel text to four alternating segments. All content from both original speeches is preserved; only the sequencing changes. Ji-ho's material splits naturally across segments 1 and 3; Emeka's across 2 and 4, keeping structural adjustment and the corruption figure together as they appeared in the original.]

**Ji-ho:** Korea in 1952 had nothing. No resources, no intact infrastructure. A war had destroyed what little had been built. What Korea had was a government willing to plan deliberately, a population accustomed to sacrifice, the geopolitical position that brought American aid and market access at exactly the right moment, and the pressure that comes from having no alternative. We built things. Textiles, then steel, then ships, then semiconductors. Each industry more complex than the last, each requiring more investment in people and institutions.

**Emeka:** Nigeria had oil. We had land, people, and a head start on virtually every metric that 1952 economists used to predict development. What we did not have was the pressure Korea had, the geopolitical position Korea had, or the favourable external conditions Korea had. We inherited colonial export contracts at independence. We fought a civil war during the critical industrialisation window. The oil was not a gift, it was an anaesthetic. It made the urgency of building disappear, it bypassed the citizen-state accountability mechanism, and it created a volatility that made long-term institutional planning almost impossible.

**Ji-ho:** We built under authoritarian rule for thirty years, and the price of that bargain is something Koreans still negotiate with our own history. But the chart is what it is. Fifty-five years later a Korean worker earns more than eleven times what a Nigerian worker earns. A Korean child born today will live, on average, thirty-two years longer. The path was hard. Parts of it I am not comfortable with in retrospect. But it existed, it worked, and other countries are walking versions of it right now.

**Emeka:** We faced structural adjustment programmes that mandated cuts to health and education exactly when investment in those areas was most needed. Four hundred billion dollars of Nigerian oil revenue was lost to corruption that the oil itself made structurally possible at that scale. None of this absolves Nigerian governments of responsibility for what they did with what they had. But it does mean the playing field was profoundly uneven, in ways the GDP chart alone cannot show you. Now we sit in 2007 at the level Korea was at in 1952. That fifty-five-year gap is not an abstraction. It is the difference between a life of dignity and a life of struggle for millions of people who deserved better.

*Final player choice:*

[A] Korea's story shows that deliberate policy and planning can overcome a difficult starting position. The model is hard but replicable for any country able to find the external support needed to make it work.

[B] Nigeria's story shows that oil dependency is a structural trap and that external conditions profoundly shape what domestic policy can achieve. Understanding the divergence requires looking at what oil prevented from being built and what external conditions made building easier or harder.

[C] Both explanations are part of the truth. The divergence cannot be explained by management alone or by structure alone, it requires both lenses held simultaneously.

## Ending A — The Planning Argument

Ji-ho's portrait, large, gold quote:

The data shows a path, and the path is replicable. Korea walked it imperfectly, under conditions I would not wish on anyone, and produced outcomes that were genuine and lasting. Vietnam began walking a version of the same path in the 1990s. Bangladesh is doing it now. Look at what these countries share — not their geography, not their colonial histories, not their natural resource endowments, all of which are different. What they share is a sequence: build manufacturing, invest in education, include women in the workforce, allow the demographic transition to happen, reinvest the dividend. External support helped Korea. I would not pretend otherwise. But the question worth asking is why Korea attracted and used that support effectively when other countries in comparable circumstances did not. The answer is that Korea had something coherent to offer: a government capable of executing a plan, institutions credible enough to attract investment, a workforce being deliberately trained for the next level of complexity. Structural conditions matter. But planning is what determines whether conditions become advantages or remain merely conditions. The chart shows you what sustained domestic commitment produces. Other countries are replicating it deliberately and consciously. That is not coincidence. That is a lesson that transfers.

[editor note: Ending A strengthened. The previous version contained "it requires favourable external conditions to be possible," which functioned as a concession that set up C. Ji-ho now addresses the external conditions question directly and subsumes it into the planning argument — the conditions were a consequence of Korea having something coherent to support, not an independent variable the model depends on.]

Ji-ho pays the tab without making a point of it. He is already thinking about the morning session. He puts on his coat and pauses at the door. Come to Seoul sometime, he says. I will show you what we built, all of it, the parts we are proud of and the parts we are still trying to reckon with. He walks out into the Swiss winter.

## Ending B — The Structural Argument

Emeka's portrait, large, green quote:

The planning argument is not wrong about what Korea did. It is wrong about why it worked. Korean governments planned well. But so did Nigerian governments — with economists, development advisers, and frameworks of their own. The question is why one set of plans produced the chart we have been looking at and the other did not. The answer is structural. Korea's planners were operating inside conditions that made deliberate planning effective: no commodity curse crowding out the sectors the plans were trying to build, geopolitical supports creating a floor underneath the risks, tax-dependent governance that gave citizens leverage over how revenue was spent. Nigerian planners were operating inside conditions that systematically undermined every plan: oil volatility wiped the budgets that funded them, Dutch Disease crowded out every alternative sector before it could grow, and oil revenue removed the accountability that makes plans stick. The oil moved through our economy like a current, displacing every other sector, hollowing out the institutions a modern state requires, bypassing the accountability between government and citizen that taxation creates. The oil made us poorer than the nothing Korea started with. That is the paradox, and it is not a paradox that better planning could have solved — because the oil is precisely what was disabling the planning mechanism. Understanding the divergence means understanding the mechanism underneath the decisions. The mechanism is structural. Everything else is downstream of that.

[editor note: Ending B strengthened. Emeka now pre-empts the planning counter-argument directly — acknowledging that Nigerian governments also planned and explaining why structure determined what planning could accomplish — rather than leaving it as an implicit gap C fills. The oil-as-current imagery from the original is preserved; the corruption figure and "$400B" are intentionally omitted here since they appear in the Final Dissertation immediately before this ending, and Emeka's closing argument is more effective at this level of abstraction.]

Emeka walks you to the entrance of the hotel. The Alps are somewhere out there in the dark. The data is accurate, he says, all of it. But data shows you the outcome, it rarely shows you the mechanism. The mechanism, the way oil structured governance, crowded out institutions, made urgency disappear, and operated within an international system that imposed different conditions on different countries, that is what you need to read underneath the numbers. He shakes your hand and goes back inside. You stand in the cold for a moment with your notebook. You have a different paper to write now than the one you arrived with.

## Ending C — The Open Question

Both portraits side by side. Alternating quotes:

**Ji-ho:** Management made the difference.
**Emeka:** Structure determined what management was possible.
**Ji-ho:** The path existed.
**Emeka:** The path was not equally available.
**Ji-ho:** The results are real.
**Emeka:** The conditions were not equal.
**Both:** Both things are true.

They walk out together, still talking. Ji-ho is saying something about institutional capacity. Emeka is saying something about colonial trade structures and the IMF. Their voices fade down the corridor. Your notebook is still open. The final chart loads on screen, the connected scatter from Chapter Three, Korea's long arc and Nigeria's short one. Then slowly the paths extend forward past 2007, drawn as dashed lines moving into a future the Gapminder data has not yet recorded. The paths are still moving. The story is not over.

The divergence between South Korea and Nigeria is real, documented, and deeply consequential for the people who lived inside it. The reasons for it are multiple, structural, and contested. What comes next, for both countries and for the dozens of others whose trajectories this data only begins to capture, has not been determined yet.
